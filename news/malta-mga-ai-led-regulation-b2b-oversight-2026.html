Malta pivots to AI-led gambling regulation

The Malta Gaming Authority's 2025 report signals a shift toward AI-led supervision of B2C and B2B licences as the market consolidates.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
28 July 2026

The Malta Gaming Authority has used its 2025 annual report to set out a supervisory philosophy that leans on data and automation, describing a pivot toward smarter, AI-led regulation of both business-to-consumer and business-to-business licences. For a jurisdiction that remains one of the industry's most important hubs, the shift is less a rebranding than a statement about how the regulator intends to supervise a large and complex licensee base with finite resources.

The economic stakes are considerable. Malta's gaming industry generated 1.42 billion euros in gross value added in 2025, up 3.5 percent on the prior year and accounting for 6.3 percent of national GDP. A sector of that weight cannot be supervised effectively through manual case-by-case review alone, and the move toward AI-assisted oversight reflects a practical recognition that the regulator's analytical capacity must scale with the market it polices. Automation, in this framing, is a tool of proportionate supervision rather than a novelty.

The report also documents a market in consolidation. The number of online operators holding a Maltese licence fell 3.3 percent to 294 companies, while active gaming accounts dropped 65.3 percent to 13.8 million and new registrations fell 75.4 percent to roughly 4.1 million. Those figures describe a market that is contracting in participants and accounts even as it grows in value, suggesting a maturing sector in which fewer, larger and better-capitalised operators command a rising share of activity.

That consolidation has licensing consequences. A regulator overseeing a smaller cohort of more substantial operators can afford deeper, more continuous scrutiny of each, and AI-led monitoring is well suited to that model. For applicants, the implication is that a Maltese licence increasingly signals membership of a supervised elite rather than entry to a crowded field, and the bar for demonstrating substance, governance and financial standing is likely to rise accordingly.

The authority's emphasis on B2B governance is a distinctive feature of the Maltese approach. Malta has positioned itself at the forefront of regulating the supplier layer, and extending AI-led supervision to B2B licensees signals that the technical and platform providers underpinning the industry will face oversight comparable to the consumer-facing operators they serve. For suppliers accustomed to lighter regulatory attention, the message is that accountability now reaches into the infrastructure of the market.

The supervisory sharpening comes as the MGA confronts a persistent integrity threat. The authority has drawn attention to a pattern of unlicensed operators impersonating MGA-licensed brands, flagging active sites including one mimicking the online arm of a well-known St Julian's casino. AI-led monitoring offers a plausible answer to that problem, since automated detection of impersonating domains and anomalous patterns is precisely the kind of task where machine analysis outperforms manual review, and it aligns the regulator's tools with the threats it faces.

For legal advisers, including Malta-based practices such as Camilleri Preziosi, WH Partners and GVZH Advocates, the report signals a regime in which licensing and ongoing supervision are becoming more data-intensive and more demanding. Operators and suppliers will need to ensure their reporting, systems and governance can withstand automated scrutiny, and applicants should expect a process that rewards genuine substance. Malta's proposition remains its credibility, and the AI-led turn is an attempt to defend that credibility as the market consolidates around it.
