Dutch KSA targets the plumbing of illegal gambling

The KSA's 2026 agenda moves enforcement beyond operators to the payment, hosting and advertising infrastructure that sustains illegal gambling.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
31 July 2026

The Netherlands' Kansspelautoriteit has set out a 2026 supervisory agenda that marks a shift in enforcement strategy. Rather than concentrating solely on the operators that offer illegal gambling to Dutch consumers, the regulator has signalled that it will devote more of its resources to the infrastructure that sustains those operators: the payment providers, hosting firms and social media platforms whose services make unlicensed gambling commercially viable. The move follows the logic that the most efficient way to disrupt an illegal market is to attack its supporting services rather than pursue each operator individually.

The agenda rests on five stated priorities: tackling illegal supply, protecting vulnerable groups, monitoring the duty of care, overseeing advertising, and enforcing the anti-money-laundering obligations under the Wwft. The overarching numerical objective is to keep at least 90 per cent of players with legal operators while reducing the revenue that illegal sites extract from Dutch consumers. The infrastructure focus is the instrument through which the KSA intends to achieve the second half of that goal, on the reasoning that starving illegal operators of payments and reach is more effective than chasing domains.

Targeting infrastructure raises novel legal questions. Payment institutions, hosting providers and platforms are not gambling operators and do not hold Dutch gambling licences, so the basis on which the KSA can compel them to act requires careful articulation. The regulator's approach is likely to combine formal and informal pressure: identifying the intermediaries that facilitate illegal gambling, engaging them directly, and, where necessary, deploying its enforcement powers or co-ordinating with the financial supervisor and other authorities. For the intermediaries concerned, the practical consequence is a heightened need to know whether the gambling businesses they serve hold Dutch authorisation.

The youth-protection strand of the agenda is equally consequential. The KSA has identified the reduction of gambling exposure and participation among minors and those aged 18 to 24 as a core priority, and it expects operators to apply the best available measures to keep untargeted online advertising away from those groups. This is a demanding standard, because it holds operators responsible not merely for avoiding deliberate targeting of the young but for the incidental reach of their advertising. Operators will need to evidence the technical measures they deploy to exclude younger audiences from their digital marketing.

The duty-of-care limb signals intensified scrutiny of licensed operators themselves. The regulator intends to examine more closely how operators discharge their statutory duty of care, including their compliance with rules on player protection, spending limits and behavioural monitoring. This dual focus, on the illegal market on one hand and on the conduct of licensees on the other, is characteristic of a maturing regime: the KSA is simultaneously defending the boundary of the licensed market and raising the standards required to operate within it. Licensees should not read the infrastructure campaign as a signal that their own compliance will attract less attention.

The advertising and Wwft priorities complete the picture. On advertising, the KSA expects stricter application of the best-available-measures standard to prevent untargeted online promotion from reaching minors and young adults, building on the restrictions already imposed since the market opened. On money laundering, the regulator's supervision of Wwft compliance places gambling operators squarely within the Dutch financial-crime framework, with obligations around customer due diligence, transaction monitoring and suspicious-activity reporting that the KSA will examine as part of its ordinary supervisory work.

For counsel, the agenda demands attention from two distinct client groups. Licensed operators should review their duty-of-care frameworks, their advertising controls, particularly the measures that keep marketing away from younger audiences, and their Wwft programmes, because each is a stated supervisory priority. Payment providers, hosting firms and platforms with any exposure to the Dutch market should assess whether they are inadvertently facilitating illegal gambling, because the KSA's infrastructure focus brings them within the enforcement frame for the first time. The Dutch regulator is following the money and the plumbing, and both sets of clients need to understand where that leads.
