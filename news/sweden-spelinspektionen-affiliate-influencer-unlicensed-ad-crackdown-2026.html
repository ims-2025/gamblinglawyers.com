Sweden targets affiliates in unlicensed gambling crackdown

Sweden's Spelinspektionen is targeting affiliates and influencers promoting unlicensed gambling, as reforms extend the law's reach to offshore operators.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
24 July 2026

Sweden's gambling regulator, Spelinspektionen, has trained its enforcement attention on the promotional ecosystem that sustains unlicensed operators, flagging affiliates, social-media channels, and influencers as vectors through which prohibited gambling reaches Swedish consumers. The shift reflects a growing recognition among European regulators that suppressing unlicensed operators requires attacking not only the operators themselves but the marketing intermediaries whose reach makes those operators commercially viable. For the affiliate and influencer economy, it signals that promotional conduct once treated as peripheral now sits squarely within the enforcement frame.

The legal escalation is real and recent. In March 2026 an influencer received a 22-month prison sentence for economic crimes connected to the promotion of unlicensed gambling, marking the first criminal conviction of its kind in Sweden and demonstrating that the exposure for promoters is no longer merely administrative. That case has reframed the risk calculus for anyone monetising gambling-adjacent content directed at Swedish audiences, because it establishes that the promotion of unlicensed gambling can attract the machinery of the criminal law rather than a fine alone.

Underpinning the enforcement drive is a set of legislative amendments that expand the reach of the Gambling Act. The reforms tighten the definition of illegal gambling activity and extend the law's jurisdiction to offshore operators that make their services available to Swedish players even without explicitly targeting them. This is a consequential doctrinal move: by shifting the test from active targeting toward mere availability, Sweden reduces the space in which offshore operators can claim they fall outside Swedish jurisdiction, and it correspondingly widens the range of promoters who may be found to have facilitated unlawful activity.

For affiliates, the practical compliance implications are significant. An affiliate that historically relied on the argument that it merely directed traffic, without itself operating any gambling, now faces a regime in which facilitating access to unlicensed operators is itself the target of enforcement. Affiliates serving Swedish audiences will need to scrutinise the licensing status of every operator in their portfolios, because the regulator has made clear that ignorance of an operator's unlicensed status offers scant protection where the promotional relationship is ongoing and commercial.

Influencers and content creators occupy a particularly exposed position. Unlike established affiliate businesses with compliance functions, individual creators often lack the infrastructure to vet the operators they promote, yet the criminal conviction earlier in 2026 shows that individual exposure can be severe. The regulator's public emphasis on social media suggests that creators who treat gambling promotion as casual sponsored content are misjudging the legal weight Swedish authorities now attach to that activity, and advisers are counselling creators to apply the same diligence to gambling promotions that they would to any regulated financial product.

The enhanced penalty framework accompanying the reforms deserves attention from legitimate licensed operators as well. As the jurisdictional net widens and enforcement tools sharpen, licensed operators have an interest in ensuring that their own affiliate arrangements are demonstrably clean, because association with non-compliant promotional practices could imperil the licences on which their Swedish businesses depend. The reforms thus create pressure not only on unlicensed operators and their promoters but on the compliance discipline of the licensed sector that competes against them.

Sweden's approach fits a broader European pattern in which regulators increasingly treat the marketing supply chain as a legitimate enforcement target. The Swedish innovation lies in the combination of criminal exposure for promoters and an availability-based jurisdictional test for offshore operators, a pairing that other member states are likely to study. For affiliates, influencers, and the operators who engage them, the lesson is that promotional conduct directed at Swedish consumers now carries legal consequences commensurate with the operator conduct it supports.
