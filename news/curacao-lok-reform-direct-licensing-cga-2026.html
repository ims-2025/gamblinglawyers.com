Curacao's LOK reform ends the master-licence era

Curacao's LOK reform scraps the master-licence model for direct CGA licensing, mandatory local presence and a rising bar for offshore operators.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
25 July 2026

Curacao's transition to a modern licensing regime under the National Ordinance on Games of Chance, known by its Dutch acronym LOK, has fundamentally altered the calculus for operators that long treated the island as the path of least resistance into online gambling. The LOK, passed in December 2024, replaced a framework that had governed the sector since 1993 and, with it, retired the master-licence model that defined Curacao's reputation. For a generation of operators, market entry meant buying a sub-licence from one of four private master-licence holders; that route is now closed.

The structural change is the shift to direct licensing by the newly established Curacao Gaming Authority. Where the old system delegated de facto gatekeeping to private intermediaries who sold sub-licences with limited ongoing supervision, the CGA now issues licences itself and supervises holders directly. That single change transforms the regulatory relationship: operators answer to a public authority with statutory powers rather than to a commercial counterparty whose incentives were aligned with volume rather than compliance. Old sub-licences ceased to provide cover once the transition took hold, forcing operators to apply on their own account or exit.

The CGA has structured its offering around defined categories, principally a B2C gaming licence covering online casinos and sportsbooks and a B2B supplier licence for software developers and payment processors. That categorisation mirrors the architecture of established European regimes and signals an intent to supervise the supply chain, not merely the consumer-facing operator. For B2B providers accustomed to operating in the regulatory shadows of the master-licence era, the requirement to hold a distinct licence marks a meaningful expansion of accountability into the technical and payments layers of the industry.

Perhaps the most consequential substantive requirement is mandatory physical presence, in force from January 2026. Operators can no longer treat a Curacao licence as a purely paper arrangement administered from elsewhere; the regime now contemplates genuine local substance, alongside anti-money-laundering obligations and player-protection requirements that were largely notional under the old model. Substance requirements of this kind are difficult to satisfy retrospectively, and they reshape the cost base of a Curacao operation in ways that favour established groups over the thinly capitalised start-ups the island once attracted.

Early data suggests the CGA is prepared to enforce that higher bar. As of April 2026 the authority had processed roughly 140 direct licence applications, approving 87 and rejecting or shelving the remainder, a rejection rate close to 38 percent. For a jurisdiction whose historical proposition rested on near-automatic approval, a refusal rate approaching two in five applications is a decisive break with the past and a clear message that the CGA intends to differentiate Curacao from the unregulated grey market rather than compete with it on permissiveness.

The reform carries strategic consequences well beyond the island. Operators that used Curacao as a low-cost hub now face a choice between meeting the CGA's requirements, migrating to another jurisdiction, or restructuring their corporate footprint to concentrate regulated activity where substance already exists. That decision interacts with tax, banking access and payment-processing relationships, since counterparties increasingly treat the quality of an operator's licensing as a proxy for risk. A credible Curacao licence may open doors that a legacy sub-licence quietly closed.

For advisers, the LOK reform is a market-entry and restructuring mandate as much as a licensing one. Firms with cross-border gaming practices, including Gaming Legal Group and WH Partners, are fielding questions about whether to commit to the new Curacao regime or redomicile to Malta or another established hub. The answer will turn on each operator's product mix, target markets and appetite for the substance and compliance investment the CGA now demands. What is no longer available is the option the island was once known for: a licence in name that asked little in return.
