Estonia raises remote gambling tax to 7%

Estonia's remote gambling tax rises to 7% in 2026 as EMTA narrows its licensing review and a Gambling Act overhaul reshapes online rules.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
25 July 2026

Estonia has continued its gradual repricing of gambling taxation, with the rate on remote gambling and sports betting rising to 7 percent in 2026, the latest step in a staged sequence that lifted the figure from 5 percent through an intermediate 6 percent in 2024. The increases are modest in absolute terms but consequential in aggregate, and they signal a policy choice to extract more fiscal value from a sector that Estonia has historically courted with a light-touch, EU-facing licensing proposition.

The structure of the increase matters as much as its size. By moving the rate upward in measured annual steps rather than a single jump, the Estonian legislature has given operators time to adjust pricing and margin assumptions, but it has also established a clear trajectory that operators must factor into medium-term planning. A tax base that ratchets predictably upward changes the economics of thin-margin verticals such as sports betting, where the gross-revenue take is already narrow and incremental duty falls directly on operator profitability.

Estonia's appeal has long rested on the reach of its licensing framework. The regime distinguishes between an operating licence and a service licence, and licences granted in Estonia allow operators to provide gambling services across the country and, in the appropriate configuration, into the wider European market. That passporting-style utility, combined with a mature digital-government infrastructure, has made the jurisdiction attractive to operators seeking an EU foothold. Rising duty tests how much of that attraction is tax-driven and how much rests on the framework's structural advantages.

The tax changes arrive alongside a broader overhaul of the Gambling Act of 2009, with the government preparing revisions to online-gaming rules, responsible-gambling measures and advertising expected during 2026. Amending foundational legislation of this vintage is significant: it allows the state to codify supervisory practices that have developed since the original act and to align Estonian law with evolving European expectations on player protection and marketing. For operators, statutory reform introduces a period of uncertainty in which established practices may be recast as formal obligations.

Administrative practice is tightening in parallel with the legislative agenda. The Estonian Tax and Customs Board, which supervises the sector and issues and revokes gaming licences, has reportedly narrowed its interpretive approach, issuing more detailed feedback on applications and applying stricter readings of the law. A regulator that both collects the tax and controls licensing occupies an unusually integrated position, and a shift toward narrower interpretation raises the practical bar for entry even where the statutory text is unchanged.

That integration of fiscal and licensing authority is a defining feature of the Estonian model and shapes how operators should approach the market. Because the same body assesses eligibility and enforces tax obligations, compliance and fiscal planning cannot be treated as separate workstreams; weakness in one is visible to the authority responsible for the other. As the Tax and Customs Board sharpens its review, applicants face heightened scrutiny of substance, ownership and financial standing at the point of entry rather than only in later supervision.

For advisers, the combination of rising duty, statutory reform and a more exacting regulator reframes Estonia from a low-friction entry point into a jurisdiction that rewards preparation. Firms with Estonian licensing practices, including Gofaizen & Sherle and NUR Legal, are guiding operators through applications that now demand fuller documentation and a credible compliance posture. The jurisdiction remains a viable EU gateway, but the terms of entry have moved, and operators that modelled Estonia on its earlier reputation will need to revisit their assumptions on both cost and process.
