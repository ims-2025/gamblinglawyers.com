Spain reopens its Remote Gambling Act for reform

Spain's DGOJ has reopened the Remote Gambling Act, weighing cross-operator deposit limits, a B2B supplier registry and tighter identity checks.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
25 July 2026

Spain's gambling regulator, the Direccion General de Ordenacion del Juego, has launched a public consultation on a suite of proposed amendments to the country's Remote Gambling Act, signalling the most substantial reconsideration of the online framework since the market was liberalised. The consultation gathers several distinct strands into a single reform package, and its breadth suggests the DGOJ intends to recalibrate the regime across player protection, market integrity and supply-chain oversight rather than tinker at the edges.

The headline measure for operators is the prospect of cross-operator deposit limits. Spain's existing controls, like those in most European markets, are applied operator by operator, which allows a customer to circulate across multiple licensees and defeat any single limit. A cross-operator limit, aggregated at the level of the player rather than the account, would close that gap and align Spain with the direction taken in markets such as the Netherlands. It would also require a central data-sharing mechanism capable of reconciling activity across licensees, a technical and data-protection undertaking whose design will determine whether the measure is workable in practice.

A second strand targets market integrity through a proposed registry for approved B2B software suppliers. By making the licensing of consumer-facing operators contingent on sourcing games and platforms from registered suppliers, the DGOJ would extend its supervisory reach upstream into the technical supply chain and use accreditation as a lever against the illegal market. This mirrors a wider European movement toward supplier accountability, and it would give the regulator a chokepoint through which to exclude providers that also service unlicensed operators.

The reform also contemplates stricter player-identification requirements, framed in part around preventing income-tax evasion. Tighter identity and source-of-funds checks sit at the intersection of gambling regulation and fiscal policy, and their inclusion reflects a growing willingness among regulators to treat gambling data as relevant to broader financial-integrity objectives. For operators, the compliance burden lies in reconciling enhanced verification with the frictionless onboarding that commercial teams prize, a tension that has proved contentious wherever affordability and identity checks have been introduced.

Running alongside the consultation is the DGOJ's continuing confrontation with prediction markets. In May 2026 the regulator opened disciplinary proceedings against Polymarket and Kalshi and ordered internet providers to block both platforms, taking the position that betting on uncertain future outcomes without a Spanish gambling licence breaches national law. That stance places Spain among the jurisdictions treating event-contract platforms as unlicensed gambling rather than as financial instruments, and it sharpens a definitional dispute now playing out across multiple markets over where betting ends and derivatives begin.

The prediction-market question is not merely enforcement housekeeping; it bears on the reform's scope. Industry discussion in Spain has openly weighed whether prediction betting could be accommodated within the existing regulatory framework, and the outcome of the Polymarket and Kalshi proceedings will shape whether such products are folded into the licensed regime or held outside it as prohibited activity. The DGOJ's willingness to order access-blocking suggests it will not cede the definitional ground lightly.

For legal advisers, the consultation is an opportunity to shape rules that will bind the market for years, and cross-border firms such as Hassans International Law Firm and MEPLAW are likely to be engaged by operators weighing responses. The stakes are highest for groups whose acquisition models depend on multi-operator play or on supplier relationships that may not survive a registry regime. The consultation's outcome will determine not only compliance costs but the competitive shape of a market that remains one of Europe's largest, and operators would be well advised to treat the process as a substantive negotiation rather than a formality.
