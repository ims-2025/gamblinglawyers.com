Belgium hardens gambling ad ban ahead of World Cup

Belgium is enforcing its near-total gambling ad ban and inducement rules into the World Cup, as shirt-sponsorship bans widen through 2028.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
25 July 2026

Belgium's Gaming Commission has used the run-up to the 2026 FIFA World Cup to remind licensed operators that the country now maintains one of Europe's most restrictive advertising and inducement regimes, and that the tournament will not soften enforcement. The warning, issued as global attention turns to football, is less a change in the law than a signal that inspectors intend to police the existing framework aggressively during a period when marketing pressure peaks. For compliance teams, the message is that the World Cup is a heightened-risk window rather than a commercial opportunity.

The legal foundation for that stance sits in the Royal Decree on gambling advertising, whose central provisions have reshaped the market since 2023. Article 61 establishes a broad prohibition on gambling advertising, subject to narrow exceptions, while Article 60 bans bonuses and player inducements outright. The structural effect is a reversal of the usual regulatory logic: rather than permitting advertising subject to conditions, Belgian law forbids it save where an operator can point to a specific carve-out, most notably an operator's own website, its physical points of sale and, under conditions, search-engine placements.

That inversion matters for how operators must build their compliance posture. Where a permissive regime invites creative interpretation at the margins, a prohibitive one places the burden on the operator to justify every promotional touchpoint by reference to an enumerated exception. Anything that cannot be mapped to a carve-out is presumptively unlawful, and the Gaming Commission has made clear that it will treat bonus-style inducements and odds-driven messaging as precisely the conduct the decree was designed to suppress. During a tournament, the temptation to push enhanced odds, free bets and affiliate-driven content is exactly where operators are most exposed.

The sports-sponsorship dimension adds a second, longer-running constraint. Since January 2025, gambling logos have been prohibited on the front of professional sports shirts in Belgium, and the framework contemplates a further tightening: from 1 January 2028, the general prohibition is set to extend to shirt sponsorship for professional clubs more broadly. Belgium is therefore some way ahead of neighbouring markets, and the English Premier League's front-of-shirt withdrawal from the 2026/27 season shows the direction of travel across the continent. Operators with multi-market sponsorship portfolios must now manage a patchwork in which Belgian exposure carries the strictest limits.

For legal advisers, the practical questions cluster around attribution and reach. A campaign lawful in a neighbouring jurisdiction can spill across borders through streaming, social media and influencer content that Belgian consumers can access, and the Gaming Commission has signalled that it will scrutinise unauthorised practices that reach Belgian users regardless of where they originate. That territorial reach places a premium on geo-targeting controls, affiliate contract terms and the documented supervision of third-party marketers, since liability is unlikely to stop at the operator's own creative output.

The enforcement architecture also reflects a broader European pattern in which regulators pair advertising restrictions with inducement bans to address the same behavioural concern from two directions. By closing off both the promotional message and the financial incentive, Belgium leaves little room for the bonus-led acquisition models that dominate less-regulated markets. Operators accustomed to competing on welcome offers must reconceive their Belgian proposition around product and service rather than price promotion, a shift with significant commercial as well as legal consequences.

The immediate takeaway is procedural discipline. Firms advising on the Belgian market, including Stibbe, AKD Benelux Lawyers and Bird & Bird LLP, are likely to be pressed on tournament-period readiness: mapping every consumer-facing touchpoint against the decree's exceptions, tightening affiliate and sponsorship contracts, and ensuring that geo-controls hold under the traffic spikes a World Cup generates. The Commission's warning is best read not as a new rule but as a statement of intent, and the operators that fare best will be those treating Belgium's prohibitive baseline as the default rather than an obstacle to be tested.
