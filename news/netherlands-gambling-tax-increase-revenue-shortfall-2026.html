Dutch gambling tax hike misses its target

The Netherlands raised gambling tax to 37.8% but the extra revenue has fallen far short of forecast, reviving fears over channelisation.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
30 July 2026

The Netherlands has learned an old fiscal lesson in a new setting. Between 2025 and 2026 the Dutch gambling tax rose in two steps from 30.5 percent to 37.8 percent, one of the sharper increases any regulated European market has imposed in a single cycle. The revenue it produced has disappointed. Additional receipts reached only around 2 million euros in 2025 and an estimated 57 million euros in 2026, far below the projections that justified the increase. A tax designed to raise money has instead demonstrated how quickly a licensed gambling market can stop behaving as a static base to be taxed.

The mechanism behind the shortfall is not mysterious. A higher tax compresses operator margins, and operators respond by cutting marketing budgets, trimming bonuses and, in some cases, reassessing whether the Dutch market is worth serving at all. Each of those responses reduces the licensed activity from which the tax is collected. When licensed offerings become less visible and less generous, a share of demand migrates to unlicensed sites that pay no Dutch tax and observe no Dutch consumer protections. The state can raise the rate, but it cannot compel players to remain inside the taxed perimeter.

The tax increase did not arrive in isolation, which compounds its effect. From 1 July 2025 the Netherlands regulator, the Kansspelautoriteit, imposed a near-total prohibition on sports sponsorship by gambling operators, removing brand visibility from team shirts, stadium signage and athlete endorsements. Layered onto deposit-limit reforms and tightening advertising rules, the sponsorship ban narrows the channels through which licensed operators can acquire and retain customers at precisely the moment the tax rise squeezes their ability to spend. The cumulative regulatory load, rather than any single measure, is what shapes operator behaviour.

The commercial consequences are already visible. Some operators have reconsidered the viability of a Dutch licence, and the market has begun to consolidate around those with the scale to absorb thinner margins. For a jurisdiction that reopened in 2021 with ambitions of a broad, competitive licensed field, a contraction toward a smaller set of larger operators is a meaningful shift in market structure, with implications for competition, consumer choice and the durability of the tax base itself.

Underlying the numbers is a genuine policy tension that the Dutch case renders unusually stark. A government wants three things at once: revenue from gambling, strong consumer protection, and a high channelisation rate that keeps players inside the licensed system. Pushed too hard, the first two goals undercut the third, because taxation and restriction that make the licensed product uncompetitive hand an advantage to the unlicensed market. The Netherlands is discovering that these objectives are not independent levers but connected parts of a single system.

The Dutch experience echoes a pattern visible elsewhere in Europe. Sweden, which raised its own gambling tax and restricted bonusing, reported channelisation of around 84 percent in 2025 against a 90 percent target, with its regulator attributing part of the gap to measures that make licensed play less competitive. The lesson recurring across these markets is that tax and marketing restrictions carry a channelisation cost that fiscal forecasts tend to understate. Revenue models built on a fixed base overstate what an aggressive rate will actually collect.

For legal and tax advisers, including Dutch specialists such as Kalff Katz and Franssen and AKD, the shortfall creates immediate client work. Operators will want advice on the viability of maintaining a Dutch licence under the current burden, on structuring their operations tax-efficiently within the rules, and on engaging with any review of the rate that the disappointing receipts may prompt. The wider strategic question, which advisers are well placed to frame, is whether the Netherlands will read its revenue miss as a reason to press harder or as a signal that the current settings have passed the point of diminishing returns.
