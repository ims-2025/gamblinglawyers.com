Brazil imposes mandatory warnings on betting ads

A July decree forces standardised risk warnings on every betting advert and pulls agencies and influencers into the compliance net.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
1 August 2026

Brazil's federal government used the window around the 2026 World Cup to redraw the rules for betting advertising, issuing a package of decrees in the first half of July that tightens the obligations of authorised operators and, for the first time, reaches deep into the supply chain of agencies, platforms and influencers that create and distribute their marketing. From 17 July 2026, every gambling advertisement must carry one of three standardised warnings attributed to the Ministry of Finance, each addressing a distinct message about addiction, financial loss and the fact that betting is not an investment.

The mandatory-warning model marks a decisive shift away from the light-touch self-certification that characterised the opening months of the regulated market. By fixing the wording of the warnings and attributing them to the Ministry, the Secretariat of Prizes and Betting (SPA) has removed the interpretive latitude operators previously enjoyed over how prominently, and in what terms, to acknowledge risk. Standardisation of this kind is familiar from tobacco and consumer-credit regulation, and its adoption signals that betting is now being treated as a product requiring statutory health-style disclosure rather than ordinary commercial promotion.

The most consequential feature of the package is its extension of responsibility beyond the licensed operator. The rules bind parties involved in creating, placing, promoting or distributing betting advertising, drawing marketing agencies, media platforms and individual influencers into a compliance perimeter that used to stop at the operator's door. For an industry that has relied heavily on affiliate and influencer promotion to acquire customers, this is a structural change: the counterparties an operator engages are now potential points of regulatory failure, and contractual risk-allocation between them becomes a live legal question.

That extension will force a rethink of the standard marketing contract. Operators will need warranties and indemnities that oblige agencies and influencers to carry the mandatory warnings, to avoid prohibited claims and to preserve records capable of demonstrating compliance. Agencies, in turn, will seek to push obligations back onto operators for the accuracy of the underlying product information. The negotiation of these terms, and the allocation of liability for a non-compliant post by a third-party influencer, is where much of the near-term legal work in the Brazilian market will sit.

Enforcement exposure is not limited to the SPA. Brazil's consumer-protection framework, administered through the Senacon system and the network of state Procons, gives authorities and consumers independent routes to challenge advertising that is held to be misleading or to breach duties of care. Operators therefore face a layered risk: a single advertising campaign can attract scrutiny from the sectoral regulator, the consumer-protection authorities and, potentially, the courts, each applying overlapping but distinct standards. Coordinating a compliance response across those fora requires local counsel fluent in both regimes.

The timing of the reform, wrapped around a major sporting event, is itself instructive. Regulators worldwide have learned that tournament periods produce the sharpest spikes in advertising volume and in exposure of vulnerable audiences, and Brazil has chosen to set its new baseline precisely when the temptation to advertise aggressively is greatest. Operators that treated the World Cup as an acquisition opportunity now find that the same campaigns are being judged against materially stricter standards, with little transitional runway.

For the market as a whole, the advertising package should be read alongside the SPA's parallel consultation on fixed-odds authorisation and its restrictions on social-gambling features as parts of a single maturation strategy. Brazil is moving, in the space of a single year, from market-opening to market-disciplining. Operators that built Brazilian growth plans on the assumption of a permissive advertising environment will need to revise both their acquisition economics and their compliance budgets, and to do so before the next enforcement cycle tests how seriously the new warnings are meant to be taken.
