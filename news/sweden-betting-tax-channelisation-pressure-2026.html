Sweden weighs betting tax against channelisation

Spelinspektionen's Q2 crackdown on illegal operators reopens the question of whether Sweden's raised betting tax is pushing players offshore.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
1 August 2026

Sweden's gambling regulator, Spelinspektionen, has reported continued action against unlicensed operators through the second quarter of 2026, keeping alive a debate that sits at the intersection of tax policy and regulatory design: whether the country's decision to raise the betting tax has strengthened or weakened the licensed channel it was meant to protect. Channelisation, the share of gambling that takes place with licensed operators, is the metric on which the credibility of the entire Swedish model rests, and it is under quiet pressure.

The Swedish licensing system, in force since January 2019, was built on a straightforward proposition: offer a competitive licensed market taxed at a sustainable rate, and players will have little reason to seek out unlicensed alternatives. The subsequent increase in the betting tax, from the original 18 per cent to a higher rate, tested that proposition. Every percentage point of tax that is passed through to players in worse odds or thinner promotions is, in principle, a reason at the margin to gamble with an operator outside the Swedish net, where no such tax applies.

Spelinspektionen's enforcement work is the counterweight to that pressure. By pursuing payment-blocking, warnings and other measures against operators targeting Swedish consumers without a licence, the regulator seeks to raise the cost and inconvenience of the unlicensed channel and so preserve channelisation without lowering the tax. The tension is structural: fiscal policy pushes some demand towards the border, while enforcement policy tries to hold it back, and the equilibrium between the two determines how much tax revenue the licensed market can actually sustain.

For operators, the legal significance lies in how aggressively Sweden defines the reach of its licensing requirement and its enforcement tools. The question of when an operator is treated as targeting the Swedish market, through language, payment methods, marketing or currency, has real consequences for firms with pan-Nordic operations. An operator that considers itself outside the Swedish regime may find that the regulator takes a broader view, and the boundary is precisely where enforcement disputes arise.

The tax debate also has a compliance dimension that is easy to overlook. Licensed operators bear not only the direct cost of the betting tax but the indirect cost of competing against untaxed rivals for the same customers. That competitive asymmetry increases the temptation to push marketing and bonusing to the edge of what Sweden's strict promotional rules allow, which in turn raises the risk of regulatory breach. Fiscal pressure, in other words, can translate into compliance risk if operators respond to thinner margins with more aggressive acquisition.

Policymakers face a genuine trade-off rather than a technical error to be corrected. A lower betting tax would improve the competitiveness of the licensed channel and, on some analyses, raise total revenue by expanding the taxed base; a higher tax raises more per krona wagered but risks shrinking that base as play migrates offshore. Where the optimum lies is an empirical question that Sweden's own channelisation data, and Spelinspektionen's enforcement results, are steadily helping to answer.

For the international operators that treat Sweden as a bellwether for well-regulated European markets, the lesson is that tax and enforcement cannot be assessed in isolation. A jurisdiction that raises its betting tax must invest correspondingly in enforcement if it wishes to hold channelisation, and operators should read the two policies together when modelling the long-term value of a Swedish licence. The second-quarter enforcement figures are one more data point in a debate that will shape the tax settlement across the Nordics.
