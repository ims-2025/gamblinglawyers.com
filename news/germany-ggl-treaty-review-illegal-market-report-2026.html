Germany's GGL faces its treaty review moment

With a new board chair and a Treaty review approaching, the GGL's latest black-market data will shape whether Germany loosens or tightens its regime.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
31 July 2026

Germany's gambling regulator, the Gemeinsame Glucksspielbehorde, has entered a decisive phase. The appointment of Christian Hochgrebe as chair of its administrative board, effective 1 July 2026 under the authority's annual rotation, coincides with the approaching review of the State Treaty on Gambling. The combination gives the GGL both new leadership and a rare opportunity to influence the legislative framework within which it operates. How it uses its latest market data will shape the debate over whether Germany's restrictive regime is working.

The evidence the GGL has put forward is a two-edged instrument. Its recent reporting claims meaningful enforcement progress: a large number of illegal operators have withdrawn their offerings, hundreds of advertisers have ceased promoting illegal brands, and the authority reviewed thousands of websites, removing more than a thousand illegal gambling sites and hundreds of advertisers. On the GGL's own figures, online channelisation stands at around 77 per cent, which the regulator presents as evidence that its supervisory model is drawing players toward the licensed market.

Yet the same figures cut the other way in the political debate. A channelisation rate of roughly 77 per cent implies that close to a quarter of online gambling activity, measured by volume, still takes place outside the licensed market. Industry advocates argue that this residual black market is not a temporary artefact of a young regime but a structural consequence of Germany's restrictive product rules, including stake limits, deposit caps and the prohibition on certain popular casino formats. On this reading, the persistence of the unlicensed market is an argument for liberalising the product framework, not for intensifying enforcement.

The Treaty review is where these competing interpretations will collide. The State Treaty on Gambling was the product of hard-fought negotiation among the German Lander, and any revision requires their collective agreement. The review process will consider whether the current settlement, with its tight controls on online slots and its restrictive advertising regime, has achieved its objectives. The GGL's data will be central to that assessment, but the data admits of more than one conclusion, and the regulator's framing of its own record will carry weight in the deliberations.

Hochgrebe has signalled that enforcement and supervision will remain central priorities, which suggests continuity rather than a pivot toward liberalisation. That posture aligns the GGL with the Lander that favour a restrictive model and places the regulator in tension with operators and their advisers who argue that channelisation cannot be improved through enforcement alone. The regulator's institutional interest lies in demonstrating that its enforcement toolkit is effective, because a conclusion that enforcement has reached its limits would strengthen the case for the product liberalisation the GGL has resisted.

For operators licensed in Germany, the review carries real commercial stakes. A liberalisation of the product rules, particularly any relaxation of stake and deposit limits or of the restrictions on casino formats, would materially improve the economics of the licensed market and its ability to compete with offshore alternatives. Conversely, a decision to maintain or tighten the current framework would preserve the conditions that operators say drive players offshore. The review is therefore not an abstract policy exercise but a determinant of the profitability of holding a German licence.

Counsel advising operators, suppliers and affiliates with German exposure should be engaging with the review process now, whether through industry associations or direct submissions. The most persuasive contributions will marshal evidence on the relationship between product restrictions and channelisation, drawing on the experience of comparable European markets. The GGL's own data provides the starting point for that argument, but its interpretation is contested, and the operators that shape how the numbers are read will have the greater influence on where Germany's regime lands.
