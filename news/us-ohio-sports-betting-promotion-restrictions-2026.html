Ohio moves to curb tie-in sports betting promos

Ohio's regulator targets promotions tied to unrelated purchases, testing how far states can police the marketing of legal sports betting.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
1 August 2026

The Ohio Casino Control Commission has proposed amendments that would prevent sports-gaming promotions from being tied to non-gaming consumer transactions, responding to instances in which operators offered betting inducements linked to unrelated purchases. The Commission's concern is that promotions of this kind blur the line between a considered decision to gamble and an incidental reward for ordinary consumer spending, and that they can mislead consumers about the nature and cost of the offer. The proposal is a small but revealing example of how state regulators are policing the marketing of a product that many of them legalised only recently.

Ohio has been among the more assertive American regulators since its market launched, and its willingness to revisit promotional rules reflects a wider pattern. As the novelty of legal sports betting fades, state commissions are turning from the mechanics of licensing to the conduct of operators in the market, and advertising and promotion are the natural focus. The tie-in promotion, which couples a bet with the purchase of some unrelated good or service, is a marketing technique that regulators increasingly regard as manufacturing gambling demand rather than serving existing demand.

The legal interest lies in the fragmentation of the American landscape. There is no federal regulator of sports-betting advertising, and each state defines permissible promotion for itself. An operator running a national campaign must therefore reconcile Ohio's emerging restriction on tie-in promotions with the different, and sometimes contradictory, rules of the other jurisdictions in which it holds licences. Compliance becomes a matter of building campaigns to the strictest common denominator, or of geo-fencing offers state by state, both of which raise cost and operational complexity.

Ohio's approach should be read alongside enforcement patterns in other states. Indiana, for example, has continued to expand its exclusion list, adding more than a thousand individuals barred from casino and online wagering for conduct including cheating, illustrating that state commissions are active on both the consumer-protection and integrity fronts. Together these measures show a maturing enforcement culture in which regulators use the full range of their tools, from promotional rules to exclusion powers, to shape operator behaviour.

There is a structural limit to this enforcement, and it matters for how effective the Ohio rule can be. State agencies can audit, fine, suspend or revoke the licences of the operators they regulate, but they have little direct purchase on offshore sportsbooks, unlicensed sweepstakes-style products and outright fraudulent sites that ignore state law entirely. Tightening the rules on licensed operators therefore risks widening the gap in permitted conduct between the regulated market and its unregulated shadow, a familiar tension in every maturing gambling jurisdiction.

For operators, the practical consequence is that marketing innovation now carries regulatory risk that must be assessed jurisdiction by jurisdiction. A promotional mechanic that is lawful and effective in one state may draw an enforcement response in Ohio, and the reputational cost of a public rebuke can exceed the commercial value of the promotion. Legal review of promotional design, once an afterthought in a fast-growing market, is becoming a standing feature of the compliance function for any multi-state operator.

The broader significance is what Ohio's move says about the trajectory of American sports-betting regulation. The first phase, dominated by legalisation and land-grab competition, is giving way to a second phase in which states scrutinise how operators behave once licensed. Firms that built their American growth on aggressive, undifferentiated national promotion will need to adapt to a patchwork of increasingly specific state rules, and to treat each commission's evolving expectations as a live compliance obligation rather than a settled backdrop.
