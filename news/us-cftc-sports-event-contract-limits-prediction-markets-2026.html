CFTC moves to limit sports event contracts

The CFTC has proposed rules barring certain sports event contracts, sharpening the federal-versus-state fight over prediction markets like Kalshi.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
24 July 2026

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has proposed rules that would bar prediction-market platforms such as Kalshi and Polymarket from offering certain categories of sports-related event contracts, including those tied to officiating decisions and player injuries. The proposal represents the federal regulator's first serious attempt to draw substantive content limits around a product that has, until now, expanded largely through litigation and regulatory ambiguity. For an industry that has leaned heavily on the argument that its contracts are federally sanctioned commodities rather than wagers, a federal move to circumscribe the product is a consequential development.

The legal backdrop is a jurisdictional battle of unusual intensity. Kalshi and the US Polymarket app operate as CFTC-regulated venues and contend that this federal status renders them lawful in all fifty states, pre-empting the state gaming laws that would otherwise govern sports wagering. State regulators have pushed back forcefully, and the resulting litigation has produced a fractured map: courts have variously restrained state enforcement, and the CFTC has itself countersued states, asserting that their actions interfere with its exclusive regulatory domain. The proposed content rules add a new layer to this contest by suggesting that even the federal regulator sees limits to what these markets may offer.

The specific exclusions under consideration are revealing. Barring contracts on officiating outcomes and player injuries targets precisely the categories that most closely resemble the proposition bets offered by licensed sportsbooks and that raise the sharpest integrity and welfare concerns. In effect, the CFTC appears to be conceding that some event contracts are functionally indistinguishable from sports gambling, a concession that cuts against the industry's foundational claim that its products occupy an entirely separate legal category. How the final rules define the excluded categories will therefore carry outsized doctrinal weight.

For operators, the proposal creates a planning dilemma. A platform that has built product roadmaps around aggressive expansion into sports contracts must now weigh the prospect that its most gambling-adjacent offerings may be foreclosed at the federal level even as it continues to litigate state challenges to its broader model. Legal teams face the unenviable task of advising on a product whose permissibility is contested simultaneously in multiple forums, under multiple bodies of law, with outcomes that may point in different directions.

The state dimension remains combustible. Minnesota's first-in-the-nation prediction-markets ban was set to take effect at the start of August 2026, and operators sought a preliminary injunction to block it, while Arizona pursued criminal theories against Kalshi before a federal court intervened, and Michigan's regulators obtained a temporary restraining order of their own. Against this backdrop, a federal rule narrowing the permissible product could paradoxically strengthen the platforms' pre-emption argument for whatever contracts remain, by demonstrating that the CFTC is actively exercising comprehensive regulatory authority over the space.

There is a deeper structural question that the proposed rules bring into focus. If the CFTC polices the content of event contracts, it implicitly assumes the role of a gambling regulator in all but name, a role Congress arguably never designed the commodities framework to perform. Critics contend that the entire edifice rests on a semantic manoeuvre that recharacterises wagering as commodity trading, and the more the CFTC intervenes in product design, the harder that characterisation becomes to sustain. Advisers should watch closely for signals about how the Commission reconciles its commodities mandate with its emerging quasi-gaming function.

For now, the practical guidance for market participants is to treat the proposal as the opening of a decisive regulatory chapter rather than a settled outcome. The comment process will shape the final contours of what remains permissible, and well-reasoned submissions grounded in the statutory text may materially affect where the lines are drawn. Operators, sportsbooks watching from the sidelines, and their counsel all have a stake in the definitions the CFTC ultimately adopts, because those definitions will determine the competitive boundary between two rapidly converging forms of American wagering.
