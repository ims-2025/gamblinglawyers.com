Malta targets clone sites impersonating licensees

The MGA's warnings over sites impersonating licensed operators put brand protection at the centre of Malta's compliance agenda.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
30 July 2026

The Malta Gaming Authority has issued a series of public notices warning that certain websites are impersonating licensed operators or otherwise passing themselves off as authorised under Maltese regulation. The pattern is familiar but increasingly organised: fraudulent sites clone the branding, and sometimes the licence numbers, of genuine licensees in order to borrow the credibility that a Malta licence confers. For a jurisdiction whose commercial value rests substantially on the trust its badge signals, the proliferation of clone sites is not a peripheral nuisance but a threat to the core asset the Authority sells.

The legal significance of impersonation runs deeper than the immediate fraud. A gambling licence functions as a trust mark: it tells a player that an operator has been vetted, that funds are segregated, that dispute mechanisms exist and that the operator answers to a regulator. When counterfeit sites appropriate that signal, they degrade its meaning for every genuine licensee, because a player defrauded by a clone may not distinguish the fake from the real Malta-licensed brand it imitated. The harm is therefore borne twice, by the consumer who is defrauded and by the legitimate operator whose reputation is diluted.

For operators, the practical consequence is that brand protection has migrated from the marketing function into the compliance function. Licensees can no longer treat impersonation as someone else's problem to police. They are increasingly expected to monitor for cloned sites and unauthorised use of their marks, to report incidents to the Authority, and to warn their own customers, all as part of the broader duty to protect players that a Maltese licence entails. Building an internal capability to detect and escalate impersonation is becoming a baseline expectation rather than a sophistication.

The Authority's own reach has limits that shape any realistic response. The MGA can publish warnings, add offending URLs to public notices and coordinate with counterparts, but clone operators are typically located offshore and beyond direct Maltese jurisdiction. Effective disruption therefore depends on cooperation with payment providers, hosting companies, domain registrars and search platforms that sit between the fraudster and the consumer. Impersonation enforcement is, in practice, a coordination problem across intermediaries as much as a regulatory one, and it rewards operators that have relationships with those intermediaries in place before an incident occurs.

The warnings arrive as the Maltese market consolidates and the Authority modernises its supervisory posture. The MGA has signalled a shift toward more technology-led supervision of both business-to-consumer and business-to-business licences, and its certification ecosystem continues to expand, with suppliers adding certified titles to their Malta-approved portfolios. Against that backdrop, impersonation is the shadow side of a strong brand: the more valuable the Malta mark becomes, the greater the incentive for bad actors to counterfeit it. A regulator investing in the credibility of its licence must also invest in defending it.

The consumer-protection dimension ties the issue back to channelisation, the objective that underpins licensing in the first place. A player who deposits with a clone site has none of the protections the licensed system promises and no meaningful recourse when funds vanish. Every such experience corrodes public confidence in the licensed market as a whole and gives ammunition to the unlicensed sector. Impersonation, in other words, does not merely defraud individuals; it undermines the case for regulated gambling that regulators across Europe are working to sustain.

For legal advisers, including Maltese specialists such as Camilleri Preziosi, WH Partners and GVZH Advocates, the clone-site problem generates a distinct advisory practice. Operators will need help with trademark enforcement and takedown strategies, with structuring incident-response protocols that satisfy their regulatory reporting duties, and with coordinating the intermediary relationships through which offshore clones can actually be disrupted. As the Authority sharpens its expectations, the operators that treat brand protection as a governed compliance discipline will be better placed than those that discover the problem only after their customers have been defrauded.
