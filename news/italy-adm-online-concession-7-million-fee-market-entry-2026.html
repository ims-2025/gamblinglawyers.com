Italy's EUR7m online concession fee raises the bar

Italy's overhauled online regime carries a EUR7m concession fee per licence, reshaping the economics of entering one of Europe's largest markets.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
31 July 2026

Italy's reorganisation of its online gambling concessions, administered by the Agenzia delle Dogane e dei Monopoli, has settled on a headline figure that will define market entry for years: a concession fee of EUR7 million per licence, payable in two instalments of EUR4 million on award and EUR3 million on launch. For a market of Italy's scale, the fee is a deliberate statement about the kind of operator the regime is designed to admit, and it recalibrates the economics of entering one of Europe's largest regulated online markets.

The new concession structure replaces a long-extended legacy regime under which licences granted years earlier had been repeatedly prolonged. The overhaul, which drew applications from dozens of operators, grants each concession a multi-year term in exchange for the substantial upfront fee. The design reflects a policy choice to consolidate the market around well-capitalised operators and to extract significant value for the state at the point of entry, rather than relying solely on ongoing gaming taxes to capture the market's fiscal contribution.

The EUR7 million fee functions as a barrier to entry, and that is plainly part of its purpose. An operator must be confident of generating returns sufficient to recover a seven-figure entry cost before it earns anything from the Italian market, which excludes smaller operators and speculative entrants. For established international operators, the fee is a manageable cost of accessing a large and mature market; for challengers and niche operators, it may render Italian entry uneconomic. The predictable result is a more concentrated licensed market dominated by operators with the balance-sheet strength to absorb the entry cost.

The instalment structure repays close reading. Splitting the fee into EUR4 million on award and EUR3 million on launch gives operators a measure of protection, in that the second tranche falls due only when the operator is in a position to begin trading. But it also means that an operator which secures a concession and then encounters delays in launching still carries a EUR3 million liability crystallising at go-live, on top of the technical, compliance and marketing investment required to enter the market. Entry planning must treat the launch tranche as a fixed near-term obligation, not a distant contingency.

The fee cannot be assessed in isolation from Italy's wider regulatory environment. Italy maintains one of Europe's most restrictive advertising regimes, rooted in the Decreto Dignita prohibition on gambling advertising, alongside demanding responsible-gambling, anti-money-laundering and technical-certification obligations that the ADM has continued to tighten. An operator weighing Italian entry must therefore add to the EUR7 million concession fee the cost of competing effectively in a market where direct advertising to consumers is largely prohibited, which raises the practical difficulty of customer acquisition and lengthens the payback period on the entry investment.

The strategic implications extend to how operators structure their Italian presence. Given the scale of the entry cost, some operators will reassess whether to enter directly or to seek access through commercial arrangements with existing concession holders, subject to the regulatory constraints on such structures. Others will weigh Italian entry against alternative uses of the same capital in other European markets. The concession fee thus operates not only as a filter on who enters, but as a factor in the broader jurisdictional allocation decisions that multinational operators make across their European portfolios.

For counsel advising prospective entrants, the analysis is fundamentally a commercial one framed by regulatory constraints. The EUR7 million concession fee, the two-instalment timing, the multi-year term, the advertising restrictions and the compliance obligations must be modelled together to produce a realistic view of the payback period and the risk-adjusted return on Italian entry. Operators that build this analysis rigorously will make sound decisions about whether and how to enter; those that treat the concession fee as a simple ticket price, without accounting for the restrictive operating environment behind it, risk committing substantial capital to a market whose economics they have not fully understood.
