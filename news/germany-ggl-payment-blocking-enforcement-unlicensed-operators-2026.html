Germany's GGL escalates payment-blocking enforcement

Germany's GGL is expanding blacklists and payment-blocking orders against unlicensed operators, testing the reach of its interstate treaty powers.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
24 July 2026

Germany's Gemeinsame Glücksspielbehörde der Länder (GGL) has entered the most assertive phase of enforcement since it assumed nationwide authority under the Interstate Treaty on Gambling. The regulator has expanded its blacklist of unlicensed operators and intensified payment-blocking directives, confirming that it has issued formal instructions to more than 40 payment service providers to sever the money flows that sustain operators serving German players without a valid treaty licence. The campaign represents a deliberate shift from targeting operators directly toward disrupting the financial infrastructure on which they depend.

The legal foundation for payment blocking has long been one of the more contested features of the German framework. The Interstate Treaty empowers the GGL to involve payment institutions in enforcement once an operator has been formally identified as unlawful, on the theory that participating in the settlement of prohibited gambling transactions is itself a prohibited act of facilitation. Yet payment providers have historically resisted, arguing about the specificity of the orders they receive and the practical difficulty of isolating gambling transactions within vast payment flows. The current escalation appears designed to overcome that resistance through volume and procedural rigour.

For payment service providers, the enforcement wave raises acute compliance questions. A directive from the GGL creates legal exposure for a provider that fails to act, but over-compliance carries its own risks, including potential liability to legitimate merchants whose transactions are wrongly blocked. Providers operating across the European single market must reconcile the German directives with their obligations under EU payment-services law and with contractual duties owed to their own clients. The prudent course is meticulous documentation of the basis for each block and close legal review of the orders received.

Operators on the receiving end of blacklisting face strategic choices with significant legal dimensions. German administrative procedure affords avenues to challenge a listing, and operators have periodically succeeded where the GGL's evidentiary basis or procedural conduct fell short. But litigation is costly and public, and a listed operator must weigh the reputational consequences of a protracted fight against the commercial reality that payment disruption can quickly render the German market unservable regardless of the ultimate legal outcome.

The enforcement drive should be understood in the context of the broader legitimacy debate surrounding the German regime. Critics have long argued that the licensed onshore product, constrained by stake limits and other restrictions, struggles to compete with the offshore alternatives that payment blocking is meant to suppress. The GGL's answer is that vigorous enforcement is precisely what makes the regulated channel viable, and that channelling players toward licensed operators depends on making the unlicensed alternative genuinely difficult to access and fund.

There is also a cross-border dimension that advisers are watching closely. Many of the targeted operators are licensed in other jurisdictions and assert that their offerings are lawful where they are established. The GGL's position, consistent with the jurisprudence permitting member states to regulate gambling within their own territory, is that a foreign licence confers no right to serve German consumers in defiance of German law. The payment-blocking campaign is, in effect, a practical test of how far a national regulator can project its authority against operators beyond its borders by acting on the financial intermediaries within them.

For the wider European market, Germany's approach offers a template that other regulators are studying. If payment blocking at scale proves effective at suppressing unlicensed activity, it will strengthen the hand of regulators elsewhere who have hesitated to deploy the tool aggressively. Operators, payment providers, and their advisers should therefore treat the German campaign not as an isolated national episode but as a bellwether for the direction of enforcement across the continent.
