UK penalty puts slot suppliers on notice

The Gambling Commission's GBP122,835 Stakelogic settlement over slot spin-speed breaches signals sharper scrutiny of B2B game suppliers.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
30 July 2026

The Gambling Commission has extracted a GBP122,835 settlement from Stakelogic BV after the supplier's online slots ran faster than the minimum interval that British rules permit. Since 2021 the design standards for online slots have required at least 2.5 seconds between spins, a deliberate brake on the speed and intensity of play. The Commission found that Stakelogic's Tiger Temple 88 title cycled in 1.97 seconds, comfortably inside the floor the rules set. The sum is modest by the standards of operator penalties, but the target is what makes the case notable.

What elevates the settlement above a routine technical infraction is the chain of events it exposed. Stakelogic itself notified the Commission of the breach, then re-tested its entire British portfolio and identified a further fifteen games that fell short of the minimum interval, by margins ranging from a single millisecond to two-thirds of a second. The investigation traced the root cause to measurement rather than design ambition: the company had timed its games with a manual stopwatch, a method incapable of resolving the differences on which compliance turns. A control failure of that kind is harder to defend than a deliberate choice, because it implies the supplier could not reliably know whether its own products complied.

The most consequential feature of the case is who paid. Stakelogic supplies games that run on other operators' websites and apps; it does not hold player accounts or take bets directly. By pursuing the supplier rather than only the licensees that hosted the games, the Commission has reinforced a principle that has been hardening across Europe, namely that responsibility for responsible product design travels up the supply chain to the party that actually builds the product. B2B suppliers that once assumed regulatory exposure stopped at the operator's door should read the settlement as evidence to the contrary.

The substantive rule at issue reflects the logic of the whole slots-design regime. Speed of play is a recognised driver of gambling harm, and the 2.5-second interval, together with bans on features that create an illusion of control or celebrate losses as wins, is intended to slow the tempo of a product engineered to be fast. A game that spins in under two seconds is not a trivial deviation from that policy; it is a direct erosion of the harm-reduction margin the rule was designed to protect. The Commission's willingness to act on sub-second discrepancies shows how literally it now reads those tolerances.

For suppliers, the measurement failure is the lesson that generalises. The Commission's expectation is not merely that games comply, but that suppliers can demonstrate compliance through robust, auditable and repeatable testing. Reliance on a stopwatch will strike any compliance officer as indefensible once stated plainly, yet it is precisely the kind of legacy process that survives in fast-growing studios until a regulator exposes it. Suppliers should treat timing and technical measurement as a governed control with documented methodology, not as an informal quality check.

The settlement also lands amid a broader intensification of British oversight. The Commission published the third annual Gambling Survey for Great Britain on 22 July, has secured additional government funding to pursue illegal gambling, and from 29 July can compel operators to withdraw non-compliant gaming machines on written notice under a new licence condition. Read together, these developments describe a regulator widening its reach across product design, land-based machines and the illegal market simultaneously. A supplier penalty for spin speed is one data point in a rising enforcement tempo rather than an isolated event.

For legal advisers, including specialist gambling practices such as Harris Hagan, Wiggin and Northridge Law, the case sharpens several recurring questions. Suppliers will want advice on the trade-offs of self-reporting, on negotiating settlements that substitute payments in lieu of penalties for formal sanctions, and on building testing and measurement governance that can withstand regulatory scrutiny. Operators, for their part, will need to revisit the contractual warranties and due-diligence they demand of the studios whose games they host, because the Commission has now shown it will follow the product to its source.
