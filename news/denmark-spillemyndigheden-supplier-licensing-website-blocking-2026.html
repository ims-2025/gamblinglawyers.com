Denmark tightens supplier licensing and site-blocking

Denmark is seeking new supervisory powers and enforcing supplier dual-certification, as courts order hundreds of illegal-gambling site blocks.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
25 July 2026

Denmark's gambling authority, Spillemyndigheden, is advancing a set of reforms that extend regulatory control deeper into the supply chain while sharpening the tools it uses against the illegal market, positioning the country among the more assertive Nordic regulators at a moment when the region's frameworks are collectively in flux. The changes are technical in form but strategic in effect, reflecting a view that channelisation and consumer protection depend as much on who supplies the games as on who operates them.

The most structurally significant reform is the requirement, in force from January 2025, that game suppliers hold a separate licence to provide games to licensed operators in Denmark. The regime introduces a dual-certification logic: a company that both supplies games and offers business-to-consumer services must hold two distinct certifications rather than relying on a single operator authorisation. That separation formalises supervision of the B2B layer and gives Spillemyndigheden a direct line of accountability to providers that previously sat behind the operators they served.

For suppliers, the dual-certification requirement changes the commercial map. Providing content to Danish-licensed operators is no longer a matter of contractual arrangement alone; it requires the supplier itself to satisfy the regulator, submit to its oversight and bear the compliance cost of certification. The practical consequence is that operators must audit their supplier relationships to confirm that every provider in the chain holds the necessary Danish authorisation, since a gap upstream can compromise the operator's own position.

Spillemyndigheden is simultaneously seeking to expand its own supervisory and technical powers. In January 2026 the authority notified the European Commission, through the Technical Regulation Information System, of proposed new powers, triggering the EU-mandated standstill and review process that governs national technical rules affecting the single market. That notification is procedurally important: it subjects Denmark's proposals to scrutiny by the Commission and other member states before they can take effect, and it illustrates the extent to which national gambling reform now runs through European channels.

On the enforcement side, the authority's use of judicial site-blocking has intensified. In 2025 Spillemyndigheden obtained district-court orders to block 334 websites offering illegal gambling, a figure that underscores both the persistence of the offshore market and the regulator's reliance on the courts to police it. Judicial blocking carries a legitimacy that administrative action can lack, but it is also resource-intensive, and the volume of orders points to an enforcement effort operating at industrial scale rather than through occasional set-piece cases.

The authority's published work on illegal gambling situates these measures within a wider strategy that combines partnerships, website blocking and preventive outreach, including visits to upper-secondary schools. That breadth reflects a regulatory philosophy in which enforcement against unlicensed operators is paired with demand-side prevention, on the premise that channelisation is sustained not only by blocking supply but by shaping the behaviour of new cohorts of potential customers before they encounter the illegal market.

For advisers, the Danish reforms crystallise a Nordic trend toward supply-chain regulation that firms such as Schjodt and Mannheimer Swartling are helping clients navigate across borders. Operators and suppliers active in multiple Nordic markets face a growing patchwork of certification and advertising rules that resist a single compliance template. Denmark's dual-certification model, backed by an active site-blocking programme and expanding statutory powers, offers a preview of where several of its neighbours appear to be heading.
