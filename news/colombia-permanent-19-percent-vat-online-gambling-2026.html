Colombia moves to make 19% gambling VAT permanent

Colombia's government wants to make the 19% VAT on online gambling permanent, cementing one of Latin America's heaviest tax loads.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
29 July 2026

Colombia is moving to entrench a tax measure that operators had hoped was temporary. A new tax reform bill advanced by the Petro government proposes making the 19 percent value-added tax on online games of chance a permanent feature of the fiscal landscape. The measure forms part of a broader package the government projects will raise COP 21.8 trillion in 2027, and it signals that revenue from online gambling has become a structural component of the state's budget arithmetic rather than a stopgap. For a market that regulated early and has been held up as a Latin American model, the shift from temporary to permanent taxation is a defining moment.

The legal significance lies in the change of character, not the rate itself. A VAT applied as a temporary emergency measure can be planned around as a passing cost; a VAT written into permanent law becomes a fixed parameter of every operator's business model. Colombia's regulator, Coljuegos, built its reputation on legal certainty and a workable licensing regime, and permanence in the tax base is a double-edged form of that certainty: operators gain predictability but lose the hope of relief. The reform therefore tests whether the market's attractiveness rested on its regulatory clarity or on the assumption that the heavier tax layers would eventually lapse.

The mechanics of a VAT on gambling are more punishing than a headline rate suggests. Value-added tax applied at the point of player deposits or stakes falls on gross activity rather than on operator margin, so its effective bite on net revenue is far larger than 19 percent would imply. Operators must decide whether to absorb the charge, which compresses already thin margins, or to pass it to players, which weakens the competitiveness of the licensed product against unlicensed offshore sites that carry no such load. That channelisation tension is the central risk the reform creates.

The regional comparison sharpens the calculus. Peru, one of Latin America's newest regulated markets, has positioned itself with a lighter touch, applying a special gambling tax capped at 12 percent of gross gaming revenue alongside a smaller consumption levy, so that total obligations land in a more moderate band. Set against Peru's model, Colombia's permanent 19 percent VAT looks like a deliberate choice to prioritise revenue over tax competitiveness. Operators weighing where to deploy capital across the region will read the two frameworks side by side, and Colombia's permanence may push marginal investment toward lower-tax neighbours.

There is also a policy coherence question. A regime that regulated to draw players away from illegal operators depends on the licensed product remaining attractive enough to hold them. If a permanent VAT raises the cost of licensed play to the point where offshore sites regain a price advantage, the tax could erode the very channelisation the licensing system was built to achieve. The government's revenue projection assumes the market stays within the regulated perimeter, but that assumption is only as sound as the licensed sector's ability to compete on price after the tax is baked in.

For operators, the immediate task is scenario planning under an assumption of permanence. Pricing, bonus structures and marketing budgets calibrated to a temporary tax must be re-run on the basis that the 19 percent VAT is now a fixed input, and any business case built on its expiry needs rebuilding. The reform also invites engagement with the legislative process itself, because the bill is still moving through Colombia's Congress and the final contours of the measure are not yet settled.

For legal advisers, including firms with established Latin American practices such as Holland & Knight, Greenberg Traurig and Bird & Bird, the reform generates both advocacy and advisory work. Clients will need help modelling the permanent tax against regional alternatives, structuring their Colombian operations for a heavier fixed burden, and engaging with the legislative debate while the bill remains open. Colombia's decision will influence how the wider region calibrates the balance between gambling revenue and market competitiveness, and the outcome will be studied well beyond Bogotá.
