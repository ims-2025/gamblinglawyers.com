Sweden's 2026 reforms reshape licence duties

New per-licence fees, a credit-deposit ban and login-level Spelpaus checks make 2026 a pivotal compliance year for Sweden's licensees.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
30 July 2026

Sweden has made 2026 a year of structural change for its licensed gambling market, layering three distinct reforms onto operators within a single calendar. From 1 March the regulator, Spelinspektionen, introduced a new fee framework; from 1 May a ban on credit-funded deposits took effect; and from 1 August operators must run real-time checks against the national self-exclusion register on every login and deposit. Taken individually, each measure is manageable. Taken together, they amount to a coordinated tightening that touches licence economics, payment operations and player-protection systems at once.

The fee reform is more consequential than a schedule of numbers suggests. Spelinspektionen has set an annual charge of around 240,000 kronor per business-to-consumer licence and roughly 16,500 kronor for a gambling software permit, but the structural change is that supervision fees are now levied per licence rather than per corporate group. For multi-brand groups that operate several licences under one corporate roof, the shift raises aggregate costs and quietly pressures the economics of running a large portfolio of brands. The reform nudges the market toward consolidation by making fragmentation more expensive.

The credit ban addresses affordability directly. From 1 May, licensed operators can no longer accept deposits funded by credit cards, overdraft facilities, personal loans or buy-now-pay-later services, closing the routes through which players gamble with money they do not have. The measure aligns Sweden with a growing consensus that credit-funded gambling is a distinct harm vector, and it obliges operators to re-engineer their payment flows to identify and refuse credit-based funding rather than treating all deposits alike.

The Spelpaus change may impose the deepest technical burden. Sweden's national self-exclusion register has existed since the market reopened, but requiring a real-time check on every login and deposit, rather than only at account opening, converts self-exclusion from a one-time gate into a continuing obligation. An operator must now query the register at each session and each transaction, which demands authentication and payment systems that can call the register reliably and at scale. The reform closes the gap through which a player who self-excluded after opening an account could continue to play, but it requires a genuine rebuild rather than a configuration change.

These protections carry a competitiveness cost that Sweden is openly wrestling with. The regulator reported channelisation of around 84 percent in 2025 against a long-term target of 90 percent, and has attributed part of the shortfall to bonus restrictions that make licensed offerings less attractive to some players than unlicensed alternatives. The 2026 reforms sharpen the same tension: every measure that strengthens player protection also risks widening the gap that draws a slice of demand toward the unlicensed market. Sweden's policy challenge is to tighten the licensed system without eroding the channelisation that justifies it.

The reforms also reach up the supply chain. Spelinspektionen has tightened requirements for software and platform suppliers, and B2B providers are increasingly pursuing direct Swedish accreditation rather than relying on their operator clients, as EvenBet Gaming did in securing a five-year supplier licence in 2026. The trend mirrors a wider Nordic pattern in which suppliers carry their own regulatory exposure, and it means compliance planning must now account for the licensing status of technical partners, not just of the consumer-facing brand.

For legal advisers, including Swedish specialists such as Mannheimer Swartling and Delorean Advokat, the 2026 package generates advisory work across several disciplines simultaneously. Groups will need help modelling the cost of per-licence fees across their brand portfolios, operators must adapt payment systems to the credit ban and build login-level Spelpaus integration that satisfies the continuing duty, and suppliers face decisions about direct licensing. The firms that can knit these threads into a single compliance roadmap will be most useful to clients trying to absorb three reforms in one year.
