Swedish court upholds AML fines on operators

A Swedish court has dismissed appeals by Betsson Nordic, Snabbare and Spooniker against Spelinspektionen's anti-money-laundering fines.

By GamblingLawyers.com Editorial Team
28 July 2026

Sweden's enforcement of anti-money-laundering obligations in gambling has been given decisive judicial backing. The Administrative Court in Linkoping dismissed appeals brought by Betsson Nordic, Snabbare and Spooniker against penalties issued by the regulator, Spelinspektionen, with rulings handed down on 2 July 2026 and announced by the authority the following day. The court rejected all three appeals and left the penalties as originally set, including a 6.5-million-krona sanction against Betsson Nordic.

The significance of the decision lies less in the individual sums than in the judicial endorsement of the regulator's approach. When a gambling authority levies AML penalties, operators frequently test whether the courts will treat the sector's obligations with the same seriousness as those imposed on banks and payment institutions. By upholding the fines in full, the Linkoping court has signalled that gambling operators are held to a comparable standard, and that Spelinspektionen's assessments will not be readily unwound on appeal.

That judicial validation strengthens the regulator's hand across its wider supervisory programme. An authority whose penalties survive appellate scrutiny can pursue enforcement with greater confidence, and operators lose a measure of the leverage that comes from the prospect of a successful challenge. The ruling therefore reverberates beyond the three companies directly affected, recalibrating the risk calculus for every licensee weighing whether to contest an AML finding or to settle and remediate.

The AML rulings sit alongside an intensifying campaign against unlicensed gambling. A recent Spelinspektionen report identifies influencers as a prominent channel for promoting unlicensed operators to Swedish consumers, and it highlights a March 2026 criminal conviction in which an influencer received a 22-month prison sentence for economic crimes coupled with unlicensed-gambling promotion, the first known criminal sentence of its kind in Sweden. The combination of civil AML penalties and criminal exposure for promoters marks a broadening of enforcement across the ecosystem.

Spelinspektionen has continued that work into the second quarter of 2026, initiating new regulatory cases against operators suspected of offering games without a Swedish licence, while several operators corrected their conduct during processing. The pattern suggests a regulator willing to escalate but also prepared to reward remediation, an approach that pairs credible deterrence with a route back to compliance. For operators, the lesson is that early correction can shape outcomes, but only against the backdrop of an authority prepared to litigate and win.

The rulings also illuminate the maturing relationship between gambling regulation and financial-crime supervision. AML compliance in gambling has historically lagged the banking sector's, and enforcement actions that survive judicial review are the mechanism by which the two regimes converge. As Swedish courts affirm that operators must meet rigorous customer-due-diligence and monitoring standards, the practical distance between a licensed gambling business and a regulated financial institution narrows, with consequences for systems, staffing and governance.

For legal advisers, including firms with Swedish regulatory and disputes practices such as Mannheimer Swartling and Delorean Advokat, the decision reframes the advice operators receive on AML risk. With appellate challenges now demonstrably difficult, the emphasis shifts toward prevention: robust transaction monitoring, documented risk assessments and demonstrable governance. The Linkoping rulings confirm that in Sweden the cost of an AML failure is unlikely to be softened by the courts, and that investment in compliance is cheaper than the penalties that follow its absence.
